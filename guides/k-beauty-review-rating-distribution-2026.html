The median captured average is 4.8. The simple mean across the 34 snapshots is 4.84, while the review-volume-weighted mean is lower at 4.78. That difference is descriptive, not proof of a platform effect: smaller snapshots happen to carry more of the rounded 4.9 and 5.0 scores in this selected dataset.

When the per-page star shares are approximately pooled using each displayed review count, the distribution is 85.5% five-star, 9.9% four-star, 3.2% three-star, 0.7% two-star, and 0.9% one-star. These are approximate because each source page rounds its displayed star shares to whole percentages, so the reconstructed shares do not sum perfectly.

The seven snapshots below 500 records contribute only 0.3% of the combined review-record total, yet they contain four of the seven rounded 5.0 averages. The 18 snapshots with at least 5,000 records contribute 95.1% of all records and contain no 5.0 average.

What this does not mean: a lower-volume product is worse, manipulated, or unreliable. It means a rounded average has less context when fewer ratings sit underneath it. Formula, audience, launch timing, promotion, and who chooses to review can all differ.

Review attention is highly concentrated

The five largest snapshots account for 46.1% of all displayed review records. The largest ten account for 72.4%. By contrast, the bottom ten together account for just 0.97%.

That concentration matters when people compare products using only star averages. A 0.1-point difference can sit beside a hundred-fold or thousand-fold difference in review base. The score answers “what is the rounded average?”; the count answers “how much displayed rating history sits behind it?” Neither one establishes efficacy.

A practical way to read a skincare rating

  1. Read the rating and review base together. Treat “4.8 from 20,000” as a different evidence context from “5.0 from 100.”
  2. Look for the distribution. A five-star share can distinguish two products with the same rounded average.
  3. Check representation. Skin-type and concern polls describe who or what is represented, not who will get a result.
  4. Use current labels for product facts. Ratings do not prove ingredient concentration, suitability, safety, or clinical performance.
  5. Do not turn popularity into efficacy. Review volume can show attention and a broader aggregate history; it cannot establish that a product caused an outcome.

Method and limits

This report uses the 34 product-page snapshots listed on this site's review-volume ranking as of 2026-08-08. For each snapshot, the analysis aligns four displayed fields: rounded average rating, review-record count, five-star share, and capture date. The total of 322,854 is the sum of displayed review-record counts, not a count of unique people.

This is a convenience sample of products covered by this site—not a random or comprehensive sample of K-beauty. The unit is a captured product-page snapshot, so similarly named listings or repeat captures may appear. Records can overlap across products, reviewer identity is not independently verified, and promotion, visibility, product age, formula, region, and capture date can differ. No individual review text, identity, or photo is included.

All calculations are descriptive. No significance test or causal claim is made. See the editor and methodology page for the broader collection and interpretation rules.

Quick answers

Why do the captured K-beauty ratings look so high?

All 34 captured averages sit between 4.6 and 5.0. The narrow band means the one-decimal score alone does little to separate products; sample size and the five-star share add essential context.

Is a 5.0 product always better than a 4.8 product?

No. None of the 18 snapshots with at least 5,000 records averages 5.0, while every 5.0 snapshot has fewer than 5,000 records. This is a sample-size pattern, not a product-quality verdict.

Are these 322,854 verified buyers?

No. They are displayed aggregate review-record counts summed across captured product pages. The total does not establish unique people, verified purchase status, or independent product outcomes.
