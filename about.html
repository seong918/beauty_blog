About The K-Beauty Data Desk

Built by a cosmetics-loving developer who wants data to make product research more useful—not more confusing.

I like cosmetics, and I am also a developer. I built this site because shopping pages contain thousands of useful signals, but those signals are difficult to compare and easy to overread. My goal is to turn aggregate K-beauty review data into practical context that helps you ask better questions before buying.

Why I built it

A 4.9 rating alone is not very informative. How large was the captured review base? Which skin types were represented in the displayed poll? Was hydration or soothing redness the more common concern label? Does a perfect-looking score come from 100 reviews or 30,000?

I use code to line up those fields consistently, then add editorial judgment about what the numbers can and cannot support. The result is not a personal skin test or a universal recommendation. It is a clearer evidence layer for your own research.

How the analysis works

  1. Capture: I record aggregate ratings, review counts, star distributions, and displayed poll results from a Korean K-beauty retail platform.
  2. Normalize: Code maps comparable fields to the same definitions so products can be viewed side by side.
  3. Analyze: When available, a capped sample of up to 500 helpful review records is processed into anonymous topic counts and metadata-flag shares. Review text is not retained or reproduced.
  4. Edit: I check the output for unsupported conclusions, missing context, sample-size problems, and language that could be mistaken for a medical or clinical claim.
  5. Publish with limits: Every page links back to its source retailer and states the snapshot date so readers can check newer information.
The central limit: poll shares describe who selected an option in a captured display. They do not prove that a product works better for that skin type or concern. Metadata flags from a capped sample are not buyer-wide rates.

What I do not do

  • Copy, quote, or translate an individual review, reviewer name, or reviewer photo.
  • Claim that an aggregate association proves efficacy, safety, causation, or personal fit.
  • Invent ingredient percentages from label order.
  • Present synthetic illustrations as product photography or before-and-after evidence.
  • Accept payment in exchange for favorable coverage.

Corrections and updates

Retail data and prices change. The date on each page is the date of the captured snapshot, not a promise that the source is unchanged today. If a number, label, or interpretation is wrong, I will correct the page and update its modification date. You can report an issue through the public issue tracker.
